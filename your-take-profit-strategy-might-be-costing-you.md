## R, and the R multiple

A trade's risk R is the distance from the entry to the stop. A result is then quoted as a
multiple of that distance, so an outcome is comparable across instruments, position sizes
and strategies: a trade that returns twice what it risked is +2R, and a trade stopped out
at its original stop is -1R. R multiples were developed and popularised by Van K. Tharp.

## Expectancy

Expectancy is the average result per unit risked over a run of trades. Stated in R it is
the mean R multiple of the sequence, which is equivalent to

    expectancy = (win rate x average win) - (loss rate x average loss)

Two consequences the video relies on, and both are arithmetic on that formula rather than
claims about any particular market:

- A strategy can have more losers than winners and still be positive, provided the average
  winner is a large enough multiple of the average loser.
- Shrinking the average winner while leaving the average loser alone lowers expectancy,
  and can take a positive sequence negative without changing the win rate at all.

The ledgers at 009, 010, 046 and 049 are built from stated R sequences and the expectancy
printed beside each one is computed from those same bars, so the number and the picture
are the same object.

## Where take profit and stop orders actually sit

The video treats resistance, prior highs and round numbers as places orders collect, and
that is documented rather than folklore. Osler's study of stop loss and take profit orders
at a large foreign exchange dealing bank found the requested execution rates strongly
clustered at round numbers, with take profit orders clustered at round numbers more
strongly than stop loss orders. The same work found take profit orders tend to reverse
price trends and stop loss orders tend to intensify them.

The follow up paper found exchange rate moves are unusually rapid at levels where stop
loss orders are known to cluster, and that those orders are sometimes triggered in waves,
producing price cascades.

This is the basis for the liquidity area target at 008, the stall and rejection into a
level at 012 and 026, and the reaction zones at 015.

- Osler, C. L. (2003), "Currency Orders and Exchange Rate Dynamics: An Explanation for the
  Predictive Success of Technical Analysis", The Journal of Finance 58(5), 1791-1819
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1540-6261.00588
- Osler, C. L. (2005), "Stop-loss orders and price cascades in currency markets", Journal
  of International Money and Finance 24(2), 219-241
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261560604001147

## Support and resistance

Support is a level where demand is expected to be strong enough to stop a decline;
resistance is a level where selling interest is expected to be strong enough to stop an
advance. That is the definition the level lines at 008, 012, 015, 019, 026 and 043 are
drawn to.

- Support and Resistance, StockCharts ChartSchool
  https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/chart-analysis/support-and-resistance

## Trend, higher lows, and trailing behind structure

An uptrend is a sequence of successively higher peaks and successively higher troughs. An
up trend line is drawn through two or more rising lows and acts as support while price
holds above it. That sequence is what the trail at 020, 047 and 056 steps behind: each new
higher low is a new place to put the stop, and the trail only ever moves in one direction.

- Trend Lines, StockCharts ChartSchool
  https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/chart-analysis/trend-lines

## Measured move

A measured move takes the length of the first leg and projects it from the end of the
correction to give a target for the continuation. That is the construction drawn at 008,
where the first leg is traced over the bars that made it and the second is projected from
the pullback.

- Measured Move, Bullish, StockCharts ChartSchool
  https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/chart-analysis/chart-patterns/measured-move-bullish

## Average true range, and sizing a target to volatility

True range for a bar is the greatest of: the current high less the current low; the
absolute difference between the current high and the previous close; the absolute
difference between the current low and the previous close. Average true range is that
value smoothed, and it was introduced by J. Welles Wilder in New Concepts in Technical
Trading Systems in 1978. Wilder's standard period is 14. ATR measures volatility only and
says nothing about direction, which is exactly why the video uses it to widen a trail
rather than to decide one.

This is the band that opens out at 020 and the widening trail step that follows it.

## Not checked

- That most traders review entries more often than exits, and that exits are the less
  studied half of a trade, is the video's framing rather than a measured statistic. No
  survey establishing the proportion was found.
- That a particular exit style suits a particular personality, and that partials reduce
  emotional pressure, is presented as a practical observation. No controlled study
  measuring either effect was found.
- The regime split between ranging and trending markets is used as a working distinction
  rather than a formally tested classification, and the video states no proportion of time
  any market spends in either.
